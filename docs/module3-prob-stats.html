GNEST305 Module 3 — Random Variables, MLE, Bayes’ Theorem, Correlation & Least Squares Regression (2.25 Hours)

1 Why Probability & Statistics Power AI Under Uncertainty

Real-world measurements—whether flower petals or motor vibration sensors—contain randomness and measurement noise. Module 3 (Text-4: Gupta & Kapoor, Fundamentals of Mathematical Statistics) gives us the mathematical tools to estimate distribution parameters, update beliefs using Bayes’ Theorem, and predict continuous outcomes via Least Squares Regression.

GNEST305 Module 3 Syllabus Checklist
  • Basics of Probability: Random variables (discrete & continuous), expectation \(E[X]\), variance \(\text{Var}(X)\), statistical measures & summaries
  • Rules in Probability: Sum rule, product rule, conditional probability \(P(A|B)\), and Bayes’ Theorem and its Applications
  • Statistical Estimation: Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE)
  • Correlation Analysis: Linear correlation (Pearson’s \(r\), direct problems)
  • Regression Analysis: Linear regression using the Method of Least Squares (OLS)

2 1. Random Variables, Statistical Summaries & Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Suppose we model a continuous feature \(X\) (such as Iris petal_length within a species) as a Gaussian (Normal) Random Variable \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with probability density function (PDF):

\[f(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Given \(n\) independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations \(\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}\), how do we estimate the unknown parameters \(\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)\)?

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): We write the Likelihood Function \(L(\mu, \sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \mu, \sigma^2)\) and maximize its natural logarithm, the Log-Likelihood \(\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = \ln L(\mu, \sigma^2)\):

\[\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\]

Setting the partial derivatives \(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial (\sigma^2)} = 0\) yields the closed-form MLE Estimators:

\[\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}})^2\]

3 2. Bayes’ Theorem and Its AI Application: Gaussian Naive Bayes

Once we have estimated the class-conditional likelihoods \(P(X \mid C_k)\) via MLE, how do we flip the conditioning to predict the posterior probability of class \(C_k\) given a new measurement \(x\)? We apply Bayes’ Theorem:

\[P(C_k \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid C_k) \, P(C_k)}{P(x)} = \frac{P(x \mid C_k) \, P(C_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K P(x \mid C_j) \, P(C_j)}\]

  • Prior \(P(C_k)\): Base rate of class \(k\) before seeing the features (\(1/3\) each in Iris).
  • Likelihood \(P(x \mid C_k)\): Evaluated from our MLE Gaussian density \(\prod_{j=1}^d \mathcal{N}(x_j \mid \hat{\mu}_{kj}, \hat{\sigma}^2_{kj})\).
  • Posterior \(P(C_k \mid x)\): Exact probability that flower \(x\) belongs to species \(C_k\)!

4 3. Linear Correlation (\(r\)) & Least Squares Linear Regression (OLS)

In engineering and data science, we frequently need to quantify the linear association between two continuous variables \((X, Y)\) and predict \(Y\) from \(X\).

1. Karl Pearson’s Coefficient of Linear Correlation (\(r\)): \[r = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} \in [-1, +1]\]

2. Linear Regression via Method of Least Squares (OLS): We fit the line \(\hat{y}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) by minimizing the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS): \[\text{RSS}(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i))^2\] Setting \(\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_0} = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial \text{RSS}}{\partial \beta_1} = 0\) gives the normal equations with closed-form solution: \[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = r \frac{s_Y}{s_X}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\]
